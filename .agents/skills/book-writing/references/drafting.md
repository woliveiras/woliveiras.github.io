# Draft a chapter

## Place the chapter in the book's progression

Establish what readers bring to the chapter, what they will learn to do, and when they will use it. Identify the first point where each concept is needed, the conditions that affect how it applies, and the evidence readers can check.

Open by putting readers in front of the work the chapter will help them do. Show a situation, an observable difference, or a decision with consequences. Recall the prerequisite they need at that moment and make clear what reading the chapter will enable them to do. Use an opening scene when a relationship it demonstrates will matter later in the explanation. Give a brief roadmap if the chapter is long or offers several paths through the material.

## Build the explanation around what changes

When teaching unfamiliar material, show a case that makes the difficulty visible. Explain the relationship the case demonstrates, name the concept where readers will use it, and apply it to a variation. If readers need the definition to make sense of the first case, give it first. In a chapter about making decisions, establish the criteria before comparing alternatives. In a reference section, put the answer and the conditions under which it applies near the start.

For a procedure, give the starting conditions, explain the purpose of each consequential step, and show the result that confirms the interpretation. Connect adjoining sections by identifying what changed, which conditions still govern the result, and which question the new state lets readers answer. If a failure or edge case can teach something, vary one condition at a time and explain which decision it affects. Point out simplifications where they could change how readers interpret the result.

Make each section answer a recognizable question. In each paragraph, link the claim to its reason, evidence, or consequence. A short sentence can highlight a diagnosis or action. Give causal relationships enough room before moving on. When switching examples, restate the question they share and identify the condition that differs.

## Keep the voice consistent through the explanation

Choose how to address readers and use the book's established vocabulary. Use second person and direct verbs when readers have an action to take. Use descriptive prose when they need to observe how a system behaves. When a term first becomes useful, relate it to something readers already know. On later uses, keep the same name and give the brief reminder the passage calls for. If two similar terms lead to different decisions, define them together and show when the distinction matters.

Motivate readers with consequences they can verify. Put each caveat, including the condition that triggers it and why it matters, next to the decision it qualifies. Explain where analogies and simplified models apply. When results vary, describe what readers should observe and which values are illustrative.

## Choose the form that serves the reader's task

Use subheadings for actions or questions readers will want to find again. Number steps when their order affects the result. Use tables for comparisons with shared criteria, diagrams for relationships, states, or flows, and captions to direct attention. When several figures compare the same quantity or structure, use consistent scales, layouts, and labels so the differences are easy to see. Introduce code or other artifacts by explaining their purpose and starting state. Say what change to look for and how to interpret the result. Use a note for a brief aside and put an alert next to the action when a condition could change the outcome. For practice activities, give an expected result, a way to check it, or a pointer to the solution.

## Close with an application readers can return to

Return to the problem raised in the opening. State the criterion, decision, or skill readers can now use and show how to verify their work. Choose a brief recap to reinforce a relationship, a small task to test application in a new case, or a cross-reference for repeating a procedure. When the next chapter depends on this result, say what it takes as input and how it will use it.

**Original example.** “At the reference desk, we have only a photograph of the pot. To locate the object, we need to find its catalog record from that image. The number you assigned in the last chapter links the two. By the end of this chapter, you'll test the link by retrieving the record without opening the collection box.”

The closing returns to the task and checks the result. “Open a photograph without looking at the label on the box. Use the number in the image to find the record, then compare the object's shape and markings with the description. Keep the photograph and record together for the shipment check. You'll use them to tell whether a label has been moved from one object to another.”

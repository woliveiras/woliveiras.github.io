# Author brief

Use this brief to collect the material that makes authorial prose possible. It
is a source check, not a form the author must complete before every edit.

## Read the material first

Derive answers from the request, notes, draft, repository context, and supplied
evidence before asking questions. Keep rough wording, fragments, reactions, and
contradictions that reveal how the author is thinking. Do not normalize them
into article language yet.

A workable brief usually contains:

- **Trigger:** What happened, failed, changed, or created the need to write?
- **Tension:** What first looked obvious, and why was that reading incomplete?
- **Concrete detail:** Which result, command, error, decision, conversation, or
  constraint makes the point real?
- **Position:** What does the author believe, reject, recommend, or remain
  uncertain about now?
- **Reader consequence:** What should the reader understand, decide, try, or
  avoid after reading?
- **Boundary:** What is unverified, context-dependent, unresolved, private, or
  outside the article's scope?

Not every text needs all six fields. It does need enough material to avoid
substituting a generic industry introduction for the author's point.

## When information is missing

Ask only questions whose answers would change the argument or voice. Prefer a
small number of concrete questions such as:

1. What happened that made you want to write this now?
2. Which assumption or approach did you change after working on it?
3. What result, failure, command, or example can the article show?
4. What recommendation are you willing to make, and where does it stop?

If the author cannot provide the missing detail yet, produce an outline or a
partial factual draft and leave specific placeholders. For example:

`[Author: add the first result that showed the duplication report was misleading]`

Do not use vague placeholders such as `[add personal story]`. Say what evidence
the paragraph needs and why.

## Briefing modes

- **Draft from a topic:** require a trigger or tension, a concrete detail, and a
  position or intended reader consequence before writing the full article.
- **Draft from notes:** infer the brief, show unresolved gaps only when they
  block the argument, then draft.
- **Revise an existing draft:** treat the draft as the brief. Preserve its
  supplied facts, opinions, uncertainty, humor, and personal constraints.
- **Documentation or reference:** skip personal questions. Ask only for missing
  behavior, audience, versions, constraints, or expected outcomes.

The briefing ends when the source can support a specific opening, an honest
argument, and a useful ending. More questions do not automatically produce a
better voice.
